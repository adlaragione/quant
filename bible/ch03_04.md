## PART A — Chapter 3: Complete markets

### 3.1 Contingent claims and attainability
- A **contingent claim** (cash-flow) at time 1 is any vector $X = (X(\omega_1),\dots,X(\omega_K))^T\in\mathbb{R}^K$; $X(\omega_k)$ is the payoff delivered in state $\omega_k$.
- $X$ is **attainable** (replicable, **redundant**) if there is a strategy $\vartheta$ with
$$V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) = X(\omega_k),\quad k=1,\dots,K \iff A\vartheta = X.$$
  Such a $\vartheta$ is a **replicating (hedging) strategy** for $X$.
- Attainable ⟺ $X$ lies in the **column span** (image) of $A$ ⟺ the linear system $A\vartheta=X$ has a solution.

### 3.2 Complete markets

> ### ★ EXAM — Definition 37 [BOR26] (Complete one-period market)
> A one-period financial market is **complete** if every contingent claim is attainable, namely if for every $X\in\mathbb{R}^K$ there exists an investment strategy $\vartheta$ such that
> $$V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) = X(\omega_k), \qquad k=1,\dots,K.$$

Completeness = **full spanning** of future cashflows.

### 3.3 Test for completeness

> ### ★ EXAM — Proposition 38 [BOR26] (Test for market completeness) — statement
> A one-period financial market is complete **if and only if**
> $$\operatorname{rank}(A) = K.$$
> Equivalently, iff $K$ columns of $A$ are linearly independent.

- $A$ is $K\times(N+1)$ so $\operatorname{rank}(A)\le\min(K,N+1)$. Hence **$N+1\ge K$ is necessary but not sufficient** (you need enough *linearly independent* payoffs, not just enough securities).
- **Consequence (frequent MC trap):** "the market is incomplete if $N+1<K$" — TRUE. "The market is complete if $N+1\ge K$" — FALSE in general. "Complete iff rank $=N+1$" — FALSE (rank $=K$ is the condition).
- If $\operatorname{rank}(A)=K$, the columns of $A$ span $\mathbb{R}^K$: any claim is a linear combination of traded payoffs. If $\operatorname{rank}(A)<K$, some claims lie outside the span.
- Practical tests: if $A$ is square ($N+1=K$): complete ⟺ $\det A\ne0$. Otherwise row-reduce / compute minors.
- Note completeness is a property of $A$ only (time-1 payoffs), it does **not** involve time-0 prices.

### 3.4 Derivative securities used to enrich the market
- **Forward contract** (Definition 40 [BOR26]): payoff $X(T) = S(T) - F$, where $F$ is the delivery (forward) price; $F$ is set so that the initial no-arbitrage value of the contract is **zero**. In one period, NA gives $F = (1+r)S(0)$ (since $0 = \mathbb{E}^Q[(S(1)-F)/(1+r)] = S(0) - F/(1+r)$).
- **European call** (Definition 42 [BOR26]): $X(T) = \max\{0, S(T)-E\} = (S(T)-E)^+$ (strike $E$, also denoted $K$).
- **European put** (Definition 45 [BOR26]): $X(T) = \max\{0, E-S(T)\} = (E-S(T))^+$.
- Useful identity: $(S-E)^+ - (E-S)^+ = S-E$ (⇒ put–call parity).

### 3.5 Completeness and derivatives
Adding a derivative may or may not complete the market; what matters is whether its payoff is **linearly independent** of existing payoffs:
- **redundant** derivative: payoff already in span$(A)$ — does not change the span;
- **non-redundant**: enlarges the span (raises rank by 1); may turn an incomplete market into a complete one (e.g. trinomial model + one call with a suitable strike ⇒ complete).

### 3.6 Second Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing

> ### ★ EXAM — Theorem 47 [BOR26] (2nd FTAP, one period) — statement
> In a one-period financial market, the following statements are equivalent:
> 1. **both no-arbitrage and market completeness hold**;
> 2. **there exists one, and only one, risk-neutral probability**.

- NA ⇒ at least one RN probability (1st FTAP); completeness removes ambiguity ⇒ uniqueness.
- Arbitrage-free & complete ⇒ unique $Q$. Arbitrage-free & incomplete ⇒ **infinitely many** $Q$ (a convex set, open polytope).
- Consequence: in a complete arbitrage-free market every claim has a **unique** NA price.
- *Why (proof idea):* RN probabilities $q$ solve $q^T A = (1+r)(1, S_1(0),\dots,S_N(0))$, $q\gg0$. If $\operatorname{rank}A=K$, $A^T q = b$ has at most one solution. If $\operatorname{rank}A<K$, there's $z\ne0$ with $A^Tz=0$; then $q+\varepsilon z$ is another RN probability for small $\varepsilon$ (sum stays 1 because the first column of $A$ is constant $1+r$).

### 3.7 Arrow–Debreu securities
**Definition.** For state $\omega_k$ the **Arrow–Debreu security** $E_k$ is the claim $E_k(\omega_h) = 1$ if $h=k$, $0$ if $h\neq k$. It pays 1 iff $\omega_k$ occurs — perfect insurance against state $\omega_k$.

**Key equivalence:** a market is complete ⟺ all Arrow–Debreu securities are attainable. (⇒ obvious. ⇐ any $X = \sum_k X(\omega_k)E_k$ is a combination of replicable claims.)

**Interpretation of state prices:** in a complete arbitrage-free market each $E_k$ has a unique replication cost, equal to the state price
$$\psi(\omega_k) = \frac{Q[\omega_k]}{1+r}.$$
State prices are unique iff the market is NA + complete. In incomplete markets some state-contingent payoffs are not perfectly insurable and state prices are not pinned down.

## PART B — Chapter 4: No-arbitrage valuation of derivatives

### 4.1 The pricing problem
Start from an **initial** market (assumed **arbitrage-free**). Introduce a new security with payoff $X = (X(\omega_1),\dots,X(\omega_K))^T$ and time-0 price $S_X(0)$. Question: **for which $S_X(0)$ is the extended market still arbitrage-free?** Answer depends on whether $X$ is **redundant** or **non-redundant** in the initial market.

- **Redundant claim:** ∃ $\vartheta_X$ in the initial market with $V_{\vartheta_X}(1)(\omega_k)=X(\omega_k)$ ∀k ⇒ **unique** NA price.
- **Non-redundant claim:** no replicating strategy ⇒ an **open interval** of NA prices.

### 4.2 Redundant claims

> ### ★ EXAM — Proposition 49 [BOR26] (NA valuation of a redundant derivative) — statement
> If the new security is **redundant**, the following are equivalent:
> 1. no-arbitrage holds in the extended market;
> 2. any replicating strategy $\vartheta_X$ satisfies $S_X(0) = V_{\vartheta_X}(0)$;
> 3. for every risk-neutral probability $Q$ of the initial market,
> $$S_X(0) = \mathbb{E}^Q\!\left[\frac{X}{1+r}\right].$$

- **Replication approach:** price = cost of the hedging strategy (gives price *and* hedge).
- **Risk-neutral approach:** price = discounted expected payoff under *any* RN probability (computationally simpler; gives price only).
- If $S_X(0) > V_{\vartheta_X}(0)$: sell the derivative, buy $\vartheta_X$ ⇒ arbitrage (type II: positive cash today, zero at $t=1$). If $S_X(0) < V_{\vartheta_X}(0)$: buy derivative, sell $\vartheta_X$.
- This is the conceptual basis of pricing in the binomial and Black–Scholes models.

### 4.3 Uniqueness of risk-neutral valuation — Proposition 53 [BOR26]
Assume the initial market is arbitrage-free. Then $X$ is redundant **iff** $\mathbb{E}^Q[X/(1+r)]$ is the **same for every** RN probability $Q$ of the initial market.
(Redundancy ⟺ uniqueness of risk-neutral valuation. Practical test: compute $\mathbb{E}^Q[X]/(1+r)$ for the parametric family of $Q$'s — if it depends on the free parameter, $X$ is non-redundant.)

### 4.4 Super-replication
**Definition.** A strategy $\vartheta$ **super-replicates** $X$ if $V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) \ge X(\omega_k)$ for all $k$ (dominates the claim state by state). Tool for non-attainable claims.
- The seller of $X$ who super-replicates is fully protected; the minimum cost of doing so is an upper bound for the price.
- **Sub-replication:** $V_\vartheta(1) \le X$; equivalently $-\vartheta$ super-replicates $-X$. The maximum value of a sub-replicating portfolio is a lower bound.

### 4.5 Non-redundant claims

> ### ★ EXAM — Proposition 55 [BOR26] (NA valuation of a non-redundant derivative) — statement
> If the new security is **non-redundant**, the following are equivalent:
> 1. no-arbitrage holds in the extended market;
> 2. $$\max_{\vartheta \text{ super-repl. of } -X}\big(-V_\vartheta(0)\big) \;<\; S_X(0) \;<\; \min_{\vartheta\text{ super-repl. of } X} V_\vartheta(0);$$
> 3. $$\inf_{Q}\ \mathbb{E}^Q\!\left[\frac{X}{1+r}\right] \;<\; S_X(0) \;<\; \sup_{Q}\ \mathbb{E}^Q\!\left[\frac{X}{1+r}\right],$$
> where inf and sup are taken over all risk-neutral probabilities of the initial market.

- **Upper bound** = minimum cost of super-replicating $X$ (seller's price).
- **Lower bound** = maximum amount one can raise at time 0 while incurring a future liability not exceeding $X$ (buyer's price) $= \max\{V_\vartheta(0): V_\vartheta(1)\le X\}$.
- **Open interval**: prices strictly inside preserve NA; prices outside **or at the bounds** create arbitrage. (At the bound, say $S_X(0) = \min$ super-repl. cost, selling $X$ and buying the optimal super-replicating portfolio costs 0 and gives $V_\vartheta(1)-X \ge 0$, strictly positive in some state since $X$ is not attainable ⇒ arbitrage.)
- **Why an interval:** no exact hedge exists, so arbitrage only imposes bounds; incomplete markets have many $Q$'s, each giving a possible price. In practice the sup/inf over the (open) set of $Q$'s are reached at the boundary (where some $q_k\to 0$) — hence they're not attained, and the interval is open.

### 4.6 Linear programming duality
Super-replication problems are linear programs; by LP duality:
$$\min\{\text{super-replication cost of } X\} = \sup_Q \mathbb{E}^Q\!\left[\frac{X}{1+r}\right],$$
$$\max\{\text{cash raised against liability} \le X\} = \inf_Q \mathbb{E}^Q\!\left[\frac{X}{1+r}\right].$$

**Exam technique (incomplete market, 1 free parameter):**
1. Parametrize all RN probabilities: $q = q(\lambda)$, $\lambda \in (\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})$ from $q_k>0$.
2. Compute $\pi(\lambda) = \mathbb{E}^{Q(\lambda)}[X]/(1+r)$ — linear in $\lambda$.
3. Evaluate at the endpoints: NA interval is $(\min,\max)$ of $\pi(\lambda_{\min}),\pi(\lambda_{\max})$ (open).
4. Super-replication: minimize $V_\vartheta(0)$ subject to $A\vartheta\ge X$ (a 2-variable LP can be solved graphically: optimum is at a vertex where the constraints hold with equality in some states).

### 4.7 Summary
- Complete + NA: every new claim is redundant ⇒ unique NA price.
- Incomplete + NA: some claims are non-redundant ⇒ NA prices form an open interval.
- The unique-price case is the special case where the bounds coincide.

### 4.8 Takeaways
Completeness ⟺ $\operatorname{rank}(A)=K$; NA + completeness ⟺ unique $Q$; redundant claims: price = replication cost = $\mathbb{E}^Q[X]/(1+r)$; non-redundant: bounds via super-replication / extremal RN expectations.

- **A.** There exists $\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}$ such that $V_\vartheta(1)=X$.
- **B.** $\displaystyle\max_{\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}:\,V_\vartheta(1)\ge-X}\big(-V_\vartheta(0)\big)=\sup_Q\mathbb{E}^Q\Big[\frac{X}{1+r}\Big]$, where the sup is taken among all RN probabilities $Q$ of the original market, and $V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k)\ge-X(\omega_k)$ holds $\forall\omega_k\in\Omega$.

> **Solution.** $4q_1+3q_2+2q_3=2.7$ and $7q_1+4q_2+q_3=3.1$ (prices divided by 1.05), with $q_1=1-q_2-q_3$ ⇒ $q_2+2q_3=1.3$ and $3q_2+6q_3=3.9$ (the same equation: consistent, so LOP holds) ⇒ $q_2=1.3-2q_3$, $q_1=q_3-0.3$, **$q_3\in(0.3,\ 0.65)$**.

**3.** *(3 points)* Is the market free of arbitrage opportunities? Justify.

> **Solution.** Yes: there are infinitely many RN probabilities ⇒ arbitrage-free (1st FTAP).

**4.** *(7 points)* A European *best of the two assets* option on $S_1$ and $S_2$ with maturity $T=1$ is introduced: it gives the right to choose between $S_1$ and $S_2$ at maturity, so $X(1)=\max\{S_1(1),S_2(1)\}$. **5.** *(8 points)* Consider strategies $\vartheta=(\vartheta_0,\vartheta_1,0)$ that do not involve $S_2$. Describe the set of all $\vartheta=(x,y,0)$ whose final value super-replicates $X(1)$.
