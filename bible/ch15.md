## 15.1 The Black–Scholes market
Two traded securities:
- **Risk-free asset:** $B(0)=1$, $\dfrac{dB(t)}{B(t)} = r\,dt$.
- **Risky stock** under the physical (historical) measure $P$:
$$\frac{dS(t)}{S(t)}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW^P(t),\qquad S(0)=S_0, \tag{1}$$
  $\mu>0$ drift (expected instantaneous return), $\sigma>0$ volatility. The stock is a **diffusion** driven by the historical BM $W^P$ (the risk factor).

## 15.2 Risk-free asset: from returns to price
$d\ln B(t) = \frac{1}{B(t)}dB(t) = r\,dt$ (no Itô correction: $B$ has no $dW$). Integrating over $(0,\tau)$: $\ln B(\tau)-\ln B(0)=r\tau$ ⇒ $\boxed{B(\tau)=e^{r\tau}}$.

## 15.3 Solution of the Black–Scholes SDE

> ### ★ EXAM — Proposition 158 [BOR26] (Solution of the SDE) — statement **and derivation**
> The SDE for the risky asset
> $$dS(t)=\mu S(t)\,dt+\sigma S(t)\,dW^P(t),\qquad S(0)=S_0, \tag{3}$$
> with $\mu,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_+$, is solved by
> $$\boxed{S(t)=S_0\,e^{\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W^P(t)}.} \tag{4}$$

**Derivation (to be reproduced at the exam):**
1. *Why we can't integrate directly.* In integral form
$$S(t)=S_0+\int_0^t\mu S(s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma S(s)\,dW^P(s),$$
   the unknown process $S$ appears **inside** the integrals on the right, so integration alone does not solve it.
2. *Change of variable: log-price.* Let $Y(t)=\ln S(t)$, i.e. $Y(t)=\varphi(t,S(t))$ with $\varphi(t,x)=\ln x$. The coefficients of $S$ are $\alpha(t)=a(t,S(t))=\mu S(t)$ and $\beta(t)=b(t,S(t))=\sigma S(t)$. Derivatives:
$$\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}=\frac1x,\qquad\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial x^2}=-\frac{1}{x^2}.$$
3. *Itô's formula* applied to $Y(t)=\ln S(t)$:
$$dY(t)=\left[0+\frac{1}{S(t)}\mu S(t)+\frac12\left(-\frac{1}{S^2(t)}\right)\sigma^2S^2(t)\right]dt+\frac{1}{S(t)}\sigma S(t)\,dW^P(t) = \left(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)dt+\sigma\,dW^P(t).$$
   The coefficients are now **constant** (do not depend on $Y$).
4. *Integrate:* $Y(t)=Y(0)+\int_0^t\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)ds+\int_0^t\sigma\,dW^P(s) = Y(0)+\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W^P(t)$ (using $W^P(0)=0$).
5. *Back to $S$:* with $Y(0)=\ln S_0$: $\ln S(t)=\ln S_0+\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W^P(t)$, i.e. $\ln\frac{S(t)}{S_0}=\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W^P(t)$. Exponentiating:
$$S(t)=S_0e^{\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W^P(t)}.\qquad\blacksquare$$

*(Optional check: apply Itô to $\varphi(t,w)=S_0e^{(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)t+\sigma w}$ with $X=W$: $\varphi_t=(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)\varphi$, $\varphi_w=\sigma\varphi$, $\varphi_{ww}=\sigma^2\varphi$ ⇒ $dS=(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2+\frac12\sigma^2)S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW$ ✓.)*

## 15.4 Lognormal prices, normal log-returns
$$\ln S(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\ln S_0+\left(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t,\ \sigma^2t\right)\quad\text{under }P.$$
- **Continuously compounded return** $\eta(t)=\ln\frac{S(t)}{S_0}=\left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W^P(t)$ is a **generalized BM**; $\mathbb{E}^P[\eta(t)]=(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)t$, $\operatorname{Var}^P[\eta(t)]=\sigma^2t$.
- Black–Scholes ⟺ i.i.d. normal increments of log-returns; $S$ is a **geometric Brownian motion (GBM)**; prices are lognormal and strictly positive.

**Estimating drift and volatility from data:** for equally spaced dates with step $\Delta t$, $\Delta\eta(t_i)=\ln\frac{S(t_{i+1})}{S(t_i)}\sim\mathcal{N}\big((\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)\Delta t,\ \sigma^2\Delta t\big)$, i.i.d.
- sample mean $\bar m$ estimates $(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)\Delta t$; sample variance $s^2$ estimates $\sigma^2\Delta t$;
- ⇒ $\hat\sigma^2 = s^2/\Delta t$, $\hat\mu = \bar m/\Delta t+\frac12\hat\sigma^2$. (Annualize: daily data $\Delta t = 1/252$ or $1/365$, weekly $1/52$, monthly $1/12$.)

## 15.5 Moments of the stock price

> ### ★ EXAM — Lemma 159 [BOR26] (Moments of $S(t)$) — statement only
> Under $P$:
> $$\mathbb{E}^P[S(t)]=S_0e^{\mu t},\qquad\mathbb{E}^P[S^2(t)]=S_0^2e^{2\mu t+\sigma^2t},\qquad\operatorname{Var}^P[S(t)]=S_0^2e^{2\mu t}\left(e^{\sigma^2t}-1\right).$$

- First moment grows at rate $\mu$ (so $\mu$ is the expected *simple* instantaneous return; the expected *log*-return is $\mu-\frac12\sigma^2$).
- Volatility enters nonlinearly via $e^{\sigma^2t}-1$.
- *How to get them (useful for exercises):* if $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(m,v)$ then $\mathbb{E}[e^{kZ}]=e^{km+\frac12k^2v}$. With $Z=\ln S(t)$: $\mathbb{E}[S(t)^k] = S_0^ke^{k(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)t+\frac12k^2\sigma^2t}$.
- **Probabilities** (very frequent in exams):
$$P[S(T)>K]=N\!\left(\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right),\qquad Q[S(T)>K]=N(d_2)\ \text{(with } r \text{ instead of } \mu).$$
  $P[S(T)<K] = 1 - P[S(T)>K] = N(-\cdot)$.
- **Median** of $S(t)$: $S_0e^{(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)t}$ (< mean).
- **Partial expectation:** $\mathbb{E}[S(T)\mathbf 1_{\{S(T)>K\}}] = S_0e^{\mu T}N\!\left(\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(\mu+\frac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right)$ (under $Q$: $S_0e^{rT}N(d_1)$).

## 15.6 Information and filtration
Investors observe $B$ and $S$. Since
$$W^P(t)=\frac1\sigma\left[\ln\frac{S(t)}{S_0}-\left(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t\right],$$
observing $S$ ⟺ observing $W^P$. The filtration generated by $S$ coincides with that generated by $W^P$ ⇒ take $\mathbb{F}$ generated by the BM. All admissible strategies are adapted to it.

## 15.7 Investment strategies — Definition 160 [BOR26]
An **investment strategy** is an $\mathbb{F}$-adapted process $\vartheta(t)=(\vartheta_0(t),\vartheta_1(t))\in\mathbb{R}^2$ (units of $B$ and $S$).
$$V_\vartheta(t)=\vartheta_0(t)B(t)+\vartheta_1(t)S(t),\qquad\tilde V_\vartheta(t)=\frac{V_\vartheta(t)}{B(t)}=\vartheta_0(t)+\vartheta_1(t)\tilde S(t),\qquad\tilde S(t)=\frac{S(t)}{B(t)}.$$
A **simple** strategy rebalances only at finitely many deterministic times; continuous-time strategies allow continuous rebalancing.

## 15.8 From discrete to continuous self-financing
For a simple strategy, self-financing means $C_\vartheta(t)=0$ at rebalancing dates, so over $[t,t+\Delta t]$: $V_\vartheta(t+\Delta t)=\vartheta_0(t)B(t+\Delta t)+\vartheta_1(t)S(t+\Delta t)$ ⇒
$$\Delta V_\vartheta(t)=\vartheta_0(t)\Delta B(t)+\vartheta_1(t)\Delta S(t).$$
Letting $\Delta t\to0$ suggests:

> ### ★ EXAM — Definition 162 [BOR26] (Self-financing strategy, continuous time)
> A trading strategy $\vartheta=(\vartheta_0,\vartheta_1)$ is **self-financing** if
> $$dV_\vartheta(t)=\vartheta_0(t)\,dB(t)+\vartheta_1(t)\,dS(t).$$

Changes in value come **only** from price changes of assets already held; no external cash injected/withdrawn. (Integral form: $V_\vartheta(t) = V_\vartheta(0)+\int_0^t\vartheta_0\,dB+\int_0^t\vartheta_1\,dS$, stochastic integrals well-defined under integrability.)
*Warning:* by the product rule, $dV = \vartheta_0dB+\vartheta_1dS + (B\,d\vartheta_0 + S\,d\vartheta_1 + d\vartheta_1dS)$; self-financing means the bracket (rebalancing cost) is zero.

## 15.9 Self-financing in discounted terms — Proposition 163 [BOR26]
Discrete heuristic: dividing $V_\vartheta(t+\Delta t)=\vartheta_0(t)B(t+\Delta t)+\vartheta_1(t)S(t+\Delta t)$ by $B(t+\Delta t)$: $\tilde V_\vartheta(t+\Delta t)=\vartheta_0(t)+\vartheta_1(t)\tilde S(t+\Delta t)$; subtracting $\tilde V_\vartheta(t)=\vartheta_0(t)+\vartheta_1(t)\tilde S(t)$ gives $\Delta\tilde V_\vartheta=\vartheta_1(t)\Delta\tilde S(t)$.

**Proposition 163.** A strategy is self-financing **iff** $d\tilde V_\vartheta(t)=\vartheta_1(t)\,d\tilde S(t)$.
*Proof:* $\tilde S=e^{-rt}S$, $\tilde V=e^{-rt}V$:
$d\tilde V=e^{-rt}dV-re^{-rt}V\,dt=e^{-rt}(\vartheta_0dB+\vartheta_1dS)-re^{-rt}(\vartheta_0B+\vartheta_1S)dt$; since $dB=rB\,dt$ the bond terms cancel ⇒ $=\vartheta_1(e^{-rt}dS-re^{-rt}S\,dt)=\vartheta_1d(e^{-rt}S)=\vartheta_1d\tilde S$. Hence $\tilde V_\vartheta(t)=\tilde V_\vartheta(0)+\int_0^t\vartheta_1(s)\,d\tilde S(s)$. ∎

## 15.10 Discounted stock and market price of risk
$\tilde S(t)=S(t)e^{-rt}$ satisfies under $P$:
$$d\tilde S(t)=\tilde S(t)\left[(\mu-r)\,dt+\sigma\,dW^P(t)\right],\qquad\tilde S(0)=S_0.$$
**Market price of risk (Sharpe ratio):** $\nu=\dfrac{\mu-r}{\sigma}$ ⇒ $d\tilde S=\tilde S\sigma\left(\nu\,dt+dW^P(t)\right)$.
Under $P$ the discounted stock has drift $\mu-r$, so it is **not** a martingale (unless $\mu=r$). $\nu$ = excess expected return per unit of volatility.

## 15.11 Girsanov's theorem — Theorem 164 [BOR26]
Define $W^Q(t)=W^P(t)+\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}t = W^P(t)+\nu t$ and
$$L=\exp\left\{-\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}W^P(T)-\frac12\left(\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}\right)^2T\right\}.$$
Then there exists a **unique** probability measure $Q\sim P$ with density $\frac{dQ}{dP}=L$ such that $W^Q$ is a standard BM under $Q$.
Consequence: $d\tilde S(t)=\tilde S(t)\sigma\,dW^Q(t)$ ⇒ **$\tilde S$ is a $Q$-martingale.**
- $Q\sim P$ (equivalent: same null events). $\mathbb{E}^Q[Y]=\mathbb{E}^P[LY]$.
- Under $P$, $W^Q$ is a BM with drift $\nu$; under $Q$, $W^P(t) = W^Q(t)-\nu t$ has drift $-\nu$.

## 15.12 Dynamics under $P$ and $Q$

> ### ★ EXAM — Proposition 165 [BOR26] (Distribution of $S$ under $P$ and $Q$) — statement only
> | | Historical measure $P$ | Risk-neutral measure $Q$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | SDE | $\dfrac{dS(t)}{S(t)}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW^P(t)$ | $\dfrac{dS(t)}{S(t)}=r\,dt+\sigma\,dW^Q(t)$ |
> | Solution | $S(t)=S_0e^{(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)t+\sigma W^P(t)}$ | $S(t)=S_0e^{(r-\frac12\sigma^2)t+\sigma W^Q(t)}$ |
> | Mean | $\mathbb{E}^P[S(t)]=S_0e^{\mu t}$ | $\mathbb{E}^Q[S(t)]=S_0e^{rt}$ |
>
> The volatility $\sigma$ is **unchanged** when changing measure; only the **drift** changes, from $\mu$ under $P$ to $r$ under $Q$.

So under $Q$: $\ln S(T)\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\ln S_0+(r-\frac12\sigma^2)T,\ \sigma^2T\big)$; conditional on $\mathcal{F}_t$:
$$S(T)=S(t)\,e^{(r-\frac12\sigma^2)(T-t)+\sigma(W^Q(T)-W^Q(t))}.$$

## 15.13 No-arbitrage
**Arbitrage (continuous time):** a self-financing $\vartheta$ with $V_\vartheta(0)\le0$, $P[V_\vartheta(T)\ge0]=1$, $P[V_\vartheta(T)>0]>0$.

**Lemma 166:** if $\vartheta$ is self-financing and integrable, its discounted value is a $Q$-martingale:
$$\tilde V_\vartheta(t)=\tilde V_\vartheta(0)+\int_0^t\vartheta_1(s)\sigma\tilde S(s)\,dW^Q(s).$$
⇒ discounted values of admissible self-financing strategies cannot generate arbitrage.

**Why admissibility matters:** continuous rebalancing allows **doubling strategies** (arbitrarily large long/short positions) that can create zero-cost sure-profit schemes. Admissible strategies are restricted: self-financing, nonnegative discounted value, sufficient integrability, e.g. $\mathbb{E}^Q\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\tilde V_\vartheta(t)^2\big]<\infty$. These exclude pathological rules and make the FTAPs applicable.

## 15.14 First FTAP (continuous time) — Theorem 168 [BOR26]
In the BS market with admissible strategies, NA holds **iff** there exists $Q\sim P$ under which the discounted stock $\tilde S$ is a martingale.

**Proof of "∃Q ⇒ NA" (slides):** take self-financing $\vartheta$ with $P[V_\vartheta(T)\ge0]=1$, $P[V_\vartheta(T)>0]>0$. Since $P\sim Q$, also $Q[\tilde V_\vartheta(T)\ge0]=1$, $Q[\tilde V_\vartheta(T)>0]>0$. Since $\tilde V_\vartheta$ is a $Q$-martingale: $V_\vartheta(0)=\tilde V_\vartheta(0)=\mathbb{E}^Q[\tilde V_\vartheta(T)]>0$. So any self-financing strategy with non-negative payoff and positive probability of gain has **strictly positive initial cost** ⇒ no arbitrage. (Caveat: requires admissibility to exclude doubling strategies.)

## 15.15 Second FTAP (continuous time) — Theorem 170, Corollary 171
NA + completeness ⟺ ∃ **unique** EMM $Q$. **Corollary 171:** Girsanov provides a unique EMM ⇒ **the Black–Scholes market is arbitrage-free and complete.**

## 15.16 Consequences of completeness
Every European claim (with suitable integrability) can be replicated by dynamic trading in $B$ and $S$ ⇒ every derivative is **redundant** ⇒ unique NA price, obtained by (1) the replicating self-financing strategy, or (2) the risk-neutral conditional expectation of the discounted payoff.

## 15.17 Summary
GBM $dS=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW^P$; after discounting and changing measure, $\tilde S$ is a martingale under the unique $Q$; self-financing ⟺ $d\tilde V_\vartheta=\vartheta_1d\tilde S$; BS market is NA and complete ⇒ unique prices for European claims.
