## 1.1 Structure of a one-period market

- **Two dates only:** $t = 0$ and $t = 1$. At $t=0$ investors choose portfolios; at $t=1$ uncertainty is resolved and all positions are liquidated.
- **$N+1$ securities**, indexed $j = 0, 1, \dots, N$.
- **Security $j=0$ is the riskless asset (bond / bank account):**
  $$B(0) = 1, \qquad B(1) = 1 + r, \qquad r > -1 \text{ (risk-free rate).}$$
  ($r>-1$ guarantees $B(1)>0$.)
- **Securities $j = 1,\dots,N$ are risky**, with prices $S_j(0)$ at time 0 (known numbers) and $S_j(1)$ at time 1 (random).

## 1.2 Uncertainty: states of the world

- Uncertainty at $t=1$ is a **finite set of states** $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_K\}$.
- States are **mutually exclusive** and each has **strictly positive historical probability**: $P[\omega_k] > 0$ for all $k$.
- The time-1 price of risky asset $j$ in state $\omega_k$ is $S_j(1)(\omega_k)$.

> Note: in a one-period market $P$ plays almost no role in pricing — only *which* states are possible matters (every state has $P>0$). This is why risk-neutral probabilities can differ from $P$.

## 1.3 Payoff matrix — Definition 2 [BOR26]

The time-1 payoffs of all securities are collected in the $K \times (N+1)$ **payoff matrix**
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix}
1+r & S_1(1)(\omega_1) & \cdots & S_N(1)(\omega_1)\\
1+r & S_1(1)(\omega_2) & \cdots & S_N(1)(\omega_2)\\
\vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\
1+r & S_1(1)(\omega_K) & \cdots & S_N(1)(\omega_K)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
- **Rows** = states of the world ($K$ rows).
- **Columns** = securities ($N+1$ columns): column $j$ is the payoff vector of security $j$ across states.

## 1.4 Investment strategies

**Definition (Investment strategy).** In a frictionless one-period market, an investment strategy (portfolio) is any vector
$$\vartheta = (\vartheta_0, \vartheta_1, \dots, \vartheta_N)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{N+1},$$
where $\vartheta_0$ = units of the riskless asset, $\vartheta_j$ = units of risky asset $j$.

- $\vartheta_j > 0$: **long** position; $\vartheta_j < 0$: **short** position (borrowing if $j=0$, short-selling if $j\ge1$).
- **Frictionless and competitive market**: no transaction costs, no taxes, no short-selling constraints, perfect divisibility, investors are price-takers ⇒ **every** vector of $\mathbb{R}^{N+1}$ is admissible.

## 1.5 Value process — Definition 4 [BOR26]

$$V_\vartheta(0) = \vartheta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^N \vartheta_j S_j(0) \qquad \text{(initial cost)},$$
$$V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) = \vartheta_0(1+r) + \sum_{j=1}^N \vartheta_j S_j(1)(\omega_k), \quad k=1,\dots,K \qquad \text{(liquidation value in state } \omega_k).$$

In matrix form: $V_\vartheta(1) = A\vartheta$ (a vector in $\mathbb{R}^K$) and $V_\vartheta(0) = \vartheta_0 + \sum_j \vartheta_j S_j(0) = (1, S_1(0),\dots,S_N(0))\,\vartheta$.

## 1.6 Cashflow process — Definition 5 [BOR26]

$$C_\vartheta(0) = -V_\vartheta(0), \qquad C_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) = V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k),\quad k=1,\dots,K.$$
Sign convention: **cash paid out is negative** (you pay $V_\vartheta(0)$ to buy the portfolio), **cash received is positive**.

## 1.7 Law of One Price

> ### ★ EXAM — Definition 7 [BOR26] (Violation of the Law of One Price)
> A one-period financial market **permits violations of the law of one price** if there exist two strategies $\vartheta$ and $\vartheta'$ such that
> $$V_\vartheta(0) \neq V_{\vartheta'}(0)$$
> but
> $$V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) = V_{\vartheta'}(1)(\omega_k) \quad \text{for all } k = 1,\dots,K.$$
> The market **satisfies the Law of One Price (LOP)** if no such pair exists.

- Interpretation: two strategies with the **same payoff in every state** at time 1 but **different costs** at time 0. Identical future cashflows must have the same price; a pricing model must exclude this.
- **Equivalent formulation (useful in exercises):** by linearity, take $\eta = \vartheta - \vartheta'$: LOP is violated iff there is a strategy $\eta$ with $V_\eta(1)(\omega_k)=0$ for all $k$ but $V_\eta(0)\ne 0$ (a "zero payoff" portfolio with non-zero price).
- **How to check LOP in practice:** LOP can only fail if the columns of $A$ are **linearly dependent** (some security is redundant). If $A\eta = 0$ has only the solution $\eta = 0$ (i.e. $\operatorname{rank}(A) = N+1$), LOP holds automatically. Otherwise, for each $\eta \ne 0$ in the kernel of $A$ check whether $\eta_0 + \sum_j \eta_j S_j(0) = 0$; if not, LOP fails.

## 1.8 Arbitrage opportunities

> ### ★ EXAM — Definition 9 [BOR26] (Arbitrage opportunity)
> A one-period financial market **admits an arbitrage opportunity** if there exists a strategy $\vartheta$ such that:
> 1. $V_\vartheta(0) \le 0$;
> 2. $V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k) \ge 0$ for all $k \in \{1,\dots,K\}$;
> 3. and **at least one** of the following holds:
>    - (3I) $V_\vartheta(1)(\omega_{\bar k}) > 0$ for some $\bar k \in \{1,\dots,K\}$, **or**
>    - (3II) $V_\vartheta(0) < 0$.

- An arbitrage requires **no positive initial investment**, **never generates losses** at time 1, and delivers a **strict gain** either today (3II: you are paid to enter) or in at least one state tomorrow (3I).
- Equivalent cashflow formulation: $C_\vartheta(0)\ge 0$, $C_\vartheta(1)(\omega_k)\ge 0$ for all $k$, and at least one cashflow strictly positive. In vector form: $M\vartheta > 0$ (see Ch. 2, cashflow matrix), where "$>$" means "$\ge$ componentwise and $\ne 0$".
- **Two types** (useful terminology):
  - *Type I*: $V_\vartheta(0) = 0$ (or $\le0$), $V_\vartheta(1)\ge0$ with positive probability of a strictly positive payoff ("free lottery ticket").
  - *Type II*: $V_\vartheta(0) < 0$ and $V_\vartheta(1) \ge 0$ ("money today, no liability tomorrow").
- Arbitrage is incompatible with **market equilibrium**: every non-satiated investor (who prefers more to less) would scale the position up indefinitely ⇒ infinite demand.

## 1.9 No-arbitrage — Definition 11 [BOR26]

**Definition (No-arbitrage, NA).** A one-period market satisfies the **no-arbitrage condition** if it admits no arbitrage opportunities.

## 1.10 NA ⇒ LOP — Proposition 12 [BOR26]

**Proposition 12.** Every one-period market satisfying NA also satisfies the LOP.

**Proof.** Suppose LOP fails: $\vartheta,\vartheta'$ with $V_\vartheta(1)=V_{\vartheta'}(1)$ in every state and, w.l.o.g., $V_\vartheta(0) < V_{\vartheta'}(0)$. Buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one: $\eta = \vartheta - \vartheta'$. Then
$V_\eta(0) = V_\vartheta(0)-V_{\vartheta'}(0) < 0$ and $V_\eta(1)(\omega_k)=0$ for all $k$ ⇒ $\eta$ is an arbitrage of type (3II). Contradiction. ∎

- So **NA is stronger than LOP**: $\text{NA} \Rightarrow \text{LOP}$, but **not** conversely (a market can satisfy LOP and still have arbitrage — e.g. one risky asset dominating the bond in all states while columns are linearly independent).

**Counter-example (LOP but not NA).** $K=2$, $r=0$, one stock $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=(2,\,1.5)$. $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&1.5\end{pmatrix}$ has rank 2 ⇒ LOP holds. But $\vartheta=(-1,1)$ costs 0 and pays $(1,\,0.5)>0$ ⇒ arbitrage.

## 1.11 Summary of logical relations (one period)

$$\text{NA} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{LOP}, \qquad \text{LOP} \not\Longrightarrow \text{NA}.$$
Key objects: payoff matrix $A$ ($K\times(N+1)$), strategy $\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}$, $V_\vartheta(0)$, $V_\vartheta(1)=A\vartheta$, cashflows $C_\vartheta$.
